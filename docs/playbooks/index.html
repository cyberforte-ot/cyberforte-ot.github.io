Suggested route

Where to start

ENISA suggests adopting the playbooks step by step. In short, from its “Progressive adoption” section:

  1. 1
    Know your context and priorities

    Start with Trust boundaries and threat modelling to find the threats that matter most for your product.

  2. 2
    Lay an engineering baseline

    ENISA's examples: playbooks 9, 10, 13, 14, 16, 17, 18 and 20, marked “Early baseline” below, plus any others your risks call for.

  3. 3
    Broaden and strengthen

    Add the remaining playbooks that apply, and improve coverage and automation over time.

ENISA stresses that this order is illustrative, later playbooks are not less important, and legal requirements (including under the EU Cyber Resilience Act) cannot be postponed.

Secure by Design

Architectural Foundations

ENISA: “structural design choices that make a product inherently difficult to compromise or exploit.”

01 Trust boundaries and threat modelling

Draw your product, find the doors, decide which ones need a lock first.

Start hereDone 02 Least privilege

Give every person and program only the keys they need, only for as long as they need them.

Done 03 Strong identity and authentication architecture

Know exactly who (or what) is knocking, on every door, every time.

Done 04 Attack surface minimisation

Fewer doors means fewer break-ins. Close everything you do not need.

Done 05 Defence in depth

Layers, like a castle: wall, moat, gate and guards, so one failure is not the end.

Done 06 Open design

Good security still works when the attacker has read the manual.

Operational Integrity

ENISA: “human and procedural practices that maintain product security throughout development, deployment, operation, and retirement.”

07 Life-cycle management

Security is not a launch-day job. It lasts from the first sketch to the last switch-off.

Done 08 User-centric design

If the safe way is the easy way, people will take it.

Done 09 Secure coding and verification practices

Write code that is hard to get wrong, then check it automatically.

Early baselineDone 10 Logging, monitoring and alerting

Keep a diary of what matters, keep it safe, and get a tap on the shoulder when something looks wrong.

Early baselineDone 11 Configuration and change management

No silent changes. Every setting is written down, reviewed and easy to roll back.

Done 12 Incident response and recovery

Hope for the best, plan for the bad day, and rehearse it.

Done 13 Vulnerability and patch management

Default Hardening

ENISA: “the factory-shipped state of the software, ensuring that the initial configuration is as restrictive as possible.”

15 Minimisation of default services

Done 16 Restrictive initial access

Early baselineDone 17 Secure communication by default
